### F-2 / G-2 — AC-9

The test I set was whether the amendment **inverts the direction** or merely adds the word "complete". It inverts it, in three independent places:

1. The criterion states the requirement as equality with an explicit second half: "no transition it states is underivable, **and no derivable transition is absent from it**."
2. The verification states the comparison as two-directional: "compare the derived set against the lead's set **in both directions**."
3. The failure mode is named explicitly and symmetrically: "A lead stating a strict subset of the derived set FAILs, exactly as one stating an underivable transition does."

Any one of the three would carry it; all three together mean a QA agent cannot read the criterion one-directionally by accident. The amendment also fixes the *population* half, which G-3 had to carry alone in round 1: the derivation now runs "over **every** doctor row the T-26 changelog entry names as changed, not a subset of them". I verified that population against the artifact: `CHANGELOG.md:29` opens 「`sc doctor` 的三行结论现在只说自己真的查到的事」 and names IPv6（AAAA）, 节点延迟 and DNS 解析 — three rows. So AC-9's population is three, and the criterion now says so.

**Inverted-R-22 test on the amended AC-9.** Could a developer satisfy it by editing the lead to something else that is also false? Not by omission — that is the direction just closed. Not by addition — an underivable transition still FAILs. Not by shrinking the subject — the population is bound to the changelog entry's own row list, not to what the developer found convenient to derive. The residual attack surface is the derivation itself being short, and that is exactly what F-3/G-3 bind at stage 4. One shape worth naming for stage 5: equality is against the *derived* set, so a developer who under-derives satisfies AC-9 against a short table. AC-9's population clause and G-3 are the two controls; QA should re-derive independently, as AC-8 already requires for the sibling case.

**Inverted-R-22 test on the amended AC-3.** Here the amendment does introduce one new shape, and it is why G-16 exists. AC-3 quantifies over "each site the clause names". A clause naming zero sites satisfies it vacuously — the same vacuity shape I ruled *benign* for AC-5 at G-7, but harmful here, because the value of R-79's clause is precisely that it says where the price lands. The BLOCKED terminal adds a mild incentive in that direction: a developer facing an unsettleable site could delete the site from the clause and never meet a BLOCKED outcome. G-16 forecloses it by requiring both sites to stay named and the BLOCKED site to be filed with what would settle it. That is a net gain over round 1: the safety hole is closed and the criterion is *more* discriminating than before, not less — which was the standard I set for this round ("an amendment that fixes a safety hole while introducing a non-discriminating criterion is a net loss").

## 4 — The ruling you asked for: a BLOCKED FR-4 site is acceptable, on terms

**The question.** AC-3 now terminates at BC-8 where source order cannot settle a site, so coverage of at most one FR-4 site may end BLOCKED rather than verified. Acceptable?

**Ruling: yes, on the terms G-16 states.** Four grounds, in order.

1. **The direction of the error is safe, and that is decisive.** FR-4 binds the clause to state the `backslashreplace` price as **prospective** — "binding a future site rather than recording a loss any shipped build took". A site whose history cannot be read cannot support the assertion "never observable here", so the delivery must either narrow the clause or mark the site unestablished. Both weaken a claim; neither manufactures one. R-74's defect — and the defect of all eleven rows — is a sentence claiming *more* than the work establishes. A BLOCKED site cannot produce that defect. It is the one failure direction this task is structurally safe against.
2. **The precedent is not merely numerous, it is exactly this trade.** I read `.harness/operator-obligations.md` rather than taking the count on report: ids 1-6, each opened by a stage that reported **BLOCKED** and substituted nothing — id 1 (T-20, "the R-31 / R-41 / R-47 precedent"), id 2 (T-07's eight `[VM]` criteria), id 3 (T-22, "honoured a sixth time"), id 4 (T-23, "a seventh time"), id 5 (T-24, "an eighth time"), id 6 (T-30, "the R-31 / R-41 / R-47 / R-52 / R-60 / R-68 precedent and obligations 1-5, honoured a tenth time"). Every one of those rows blocked on the *same hazard class* AC-3 was amended to avoid: the installed `/usr/local/bin/sc` running as root against the live service, with obligations 3, 4 and 6 each naming the import-time re-exec (R-78's near-miss in T-25) as the reason. Ruling AC-3's BLOCKED terminal unacceptable here would break a ten-instance line of precedent on the one row where the hazard is identical.
3. **The residual risk is small in fact, not just in principle.** The BLOCKED terminal fires only if `git show` cannot settle a site's source order — and stage 4 holds a shell, the introducing commit is reachable via `git log -S 'backslashreplace' -- bin/sc` (V-3), and the sites are two named code regions in one file. I expect zero BLOCKED sites. The condition exists for the case where I am wrong, not because I expect to be.
4. **BC-2 does not bar it.** BC-2 governs a row that is a code defect wearing prose disguise and forbids weakening a sentence *to match a defect*; its standard ("a claim is narrowed only after the stronger claim is proved never to have been available") is about narrowing to accommodate broken code. A BLOCKED site is neither — it is an unread history. The correct disposition is to say so, which is what G-16 requires, rather than to narrow silently.

**The terms** are G-16, and the reason they are needed is §2's vacuity finding: without them, "BLOCKED" and "site quietly deleted from the clause" are indistinguishable in the delivered artifact, and only the first is honest. A filed row that names the site and the `git show` that would settle it is actionable by the next task or by an operator; a deleted site name is unrecoverable.
